# Complete writing guidance from the TU/e reader

Source: TU/e Language and Writing Center, *Academic Writing Reader*, 17-page edition supplied as university course material. Page references below use its printed numbering. No publication date was established from that copy.

This is a complete, independently worded transfer of its writing guidance, including the lessons in its figures, examples, footnote, and resource recommendations. It is not a transcript or a replacement copy of the university PDF. Examples here are original and hypothetical unless explicitly described as lessons from the reader. Do not use their invented measurements as evidence in a real document.

The source's recommendations are the default for this skill. Preserve its stated qualifications; do not silently downgrade a recommendation because a different writing convention is common elsewhere. If an assignment, publication, or the user specifies a different convention, apply that requirement and identify any material departure when relevant. Source guidance and additional agent operating rules are distinguished in `SKILL.md`.

## 1. Writing is deliberate communication — p. 3

The quality of an idea does not make its explanation automatically clear or convincing. Write so that the intended audience can understand and appreciate the contribution, whether that audience consists of teachers, peers, colleagues, managers, or other readers.

Treat writing as a combination of intellectual preparation and physical composition. Effective writing takes attention and conscious decisions even from experienced writers; producing sentences is only part of the work. Allow time for organizing ideas, drafting them, and evaluating the result.

## 2. The three stages and their interaction — pp. 3–4, Figure 1

### Prewriting

Discover and generate ideas; consider, collect, and organize information; plan the text. Identify the key ideas, work out their logical relationships, and develop the claims or arguments that will guide the writing. Do not begin by merely arranging whatever sentences happen to be available.

### Drafting

Express the prepared ideas, connections, claims, and arguments in sentences and paragraphs. Make those relationships clear, logical, and convincing. A draft is an opportunity to express the reasoning, not a demand for a perfect first attempt: evaluation creates further opportunities to improve it.

### Evaluating

Critically examine the draft, rethink it, revise it, and edit it. Check both how the ideas are communicated and how they are arranged and connected. Assess whether claims have been convincingly argued and defended, rather than checking language alone.

Seek feedback from one or more external readers. Their fresh perspective helps establish whether they understand the ideas as intended. Use misunderstanding as evidence about the text rather than assuming the reader should infer the intended connection.

### Iterate between stages

These stages overlap and are not a one-way sequence. Evaluation may reveal a missing source or idea and send the writer back to prewriting; a confusing passage may require another drafting pass. Quality writing involves all three stages at some point, even when the writer moves repeatedly between them.

## 3. Writing decisions and the three pillars — p. 4, Figure 2

Make deliberate decisions about the whole document's structure, paragraph structure, argumentation, style, and language features. Organize those decisions around three connected concerns:

- **Argumentation:** which ideas belong, which do not, and how they should be organized to establish the message.
- **Readability:** how to communicate the ideas so that the reader understands their intended meaning and relationships.
- **Academic style:** the degree of formality and the language conventions appropriate to the text.

All three support effective writing. Fluent sentences cannot compensate for unsupported reasoning, and strong evidence cannot compensate for an explanation the reader cannot follow.

## 4. Establish the main argument — pp. 4–5

Argumentation begins with developing ideas and the connections between them. Establish a clear overall aim before selecting supporting material. Answer these questions in substance:

- What should this text achieve?
- What is new, interesting, or valuable about its ideas for the intended reader?
- What central message should remain with the reader after finishing?

Use the answers to define the main argument: the overarching idea that directs the text's argumentation. Supporting arguments should establish ideas that connect logically to that main argument and collectively make it convincing.

### Gather information cyclically — Figure 3, p. 5

Collect relevant information and ideas, make an inventory of what is available, then look for additional sources to address what is missing. Repeat this cycle as the developing argument reveals further needs. An initial collection of sources is not necessarily a sufficient evidence base.

### Select for relevance and completeness

Include all ideas and information needed to explain and substantiate the main argument and its supporting arguments. Leave out material that is irrelevant to them: distracting information can obscure the purpose and confuse the reader even if it is interesting by itself. Necessary inclusion and deliberate exclusion are equally important.

In review-only work, identify missing or irrelevant material and explain its effect. Actual additions or removals depend on the requested editing scope.

## 5. Build supporting arguments — pp. 5–7, Figure 4

Use evidence to form assumptions and draw conclusions, then communicate the significance of that evidence. Showing why it matters demonstrates critical thinking; collecting facts alone does not.

Before incorporating a source, establish whether it relates to the main or supporting argument, why it is relevant, how it supports the point, and how that relationship can be explained convincingly to the reader. A source about the same broad topic can still fail to support the actual claim.

A supporting argument needs three components: **claim, evidence, and reasoning**. Begin with the claim. Evidence and reasoning can then be ordered or interwoven according to what makes the explanation logical; they do not require an inflexible evidence-then-reasoning pattern.

### Claim

State the central assertion of the supporting argument. It must connect clearly and logically to the text's overall aim, and it must be arguable: a reader could question or dispute it. Naming a topic is not the same as making a claim. Because readers need not agree automatically, a claim needs evidence and reasoning rather than standing alone.

Original example: “Library opening hours” is a topic; “Extending evening library access could improve access for students with daytime work commitments” is an arguable claim. The latter still needs support.

### Evidence

Evidence can include statistics, research findings, examples, expert views, case studies, and interview transcripts. Seek and assess evidence carefully before using it. Evaluate four distinct qualities:

- **Strength:** is it sufficient to make this claim convincing?
- **Relevance:** does it address what this claim actually asserts?
- **Reliability:** does it come from a trustworthy, academically appropriate source?
- **Accuracy of representation:** does the writing preserve the source's content and tone?

Weak or irrelevant evidence can cause readers to dismiss the argument. Reliability and accurate representation are separate requirements: a trustworthy source can be misrepresented, and accurate reporting of an irrelevant finding does not establish the claim. Accuracy does not mean copying source text without appropriate quotation and attribution.

Original example preserving the distinction in Text Box 1: for a claim that evening access reduces scheduling conflicts, a study measuring those conflicts is relevant. A study showing that the library's café revenue increased addresses a different outcome. Both concern the same facility, but only the first bears directly on the stated claim. Neither automatically establishes an additional claim that extending hours is the most cost-effective intervention.

### Reasoning

Explain the evidence and how, and to what extent, it supports the claim. Make the significance and connection explicit. Do not leave the reader to supply the missing interpretation, even if it seems obvious to the writer.

Without reasoning, readers may miss the relevance, misunderstand the finding, or draw a different conclusion that conflicts with the intended argument. Even a reader who independently reaches the intended conclusion should be able to identify the writer's own line of reasoning and interpretation.

Original example: evidence that most survey respondents with daytime jobs use the library after 18:00 does not speak for itself. The reasoning would explain that evening provision makes access possible outside their work hours, while distinguishing this observed use from an unmeasured improvement in academic performance.

## 6. Use paragraphs to communicate arguments — pp. 7–9, footnote 1 and Text Box 2

Supporting arguments conventionally appear in body paragraphs. As a rule of thumb, develop no more than one supporting argument in each paragraph. Most argumentative body paragraphs should use the standard structure below. The reader explicitly qualifies this model: not all paragraphs are standard paragraphs, and not every paragraph must follow it.

### Topic sentence

Begin a standard paragraph with one sentence stating the arguable claim. It signals the paragraph's main concepts and prepares the reader for the information that follows. Check that the rest of the paragraph develops the announced point.

### Supporting sentences

Develop and defend the topic sentence with evidence and reasoning. Supporting sentences may illustrate phenomena, present data, define terms, evaluate causes, and examine consequences. These functions serve the claim rather than becoming unrelated additions.

### Concluding or transitioning sentence

A **conclusion** generally restates the topic sentence's claim in different words, giving it emphasis and a sense of completion.

A **transition** introduces a new idea that is connected to and logically follows from the current claim. The next paragraph or section then develops that idea as its own topic. A transition should prepare a real next step, not announce a topic that is never taken up.

The final sentence can continue the reasoning while also functioning as a transition. These functions are not mutually exclusive.

### Worked paragraph and its functions

The following uses invented facts solely to demonstrate structure:

> Scheduled study reminders can improve participation in an optional practice programme. In a hypothetical course trial, 72% of students receiving reminders completed the weekly exercise, compared with 54% of students who did not receive reminders. This difference supports using reminders when the immediate objective is exercise participation; it does not establish that the additional participation improves learning. A separate assessment of learning outcomes would therefore be needed before recommending reminders as a way to raise attainment.

The opening sentence makes the claim and identifies the main concepts. The next sentence provides evidence with an explicit comparison. The third interprets the evidence and bounds what it establishes. The final sentence both reasons from that limitation and prepares a subsequent discussion of outcome assessment.

The reader's own annotated example similarly teaches that reasoning can evaluate an outcome, propose a response, and explain its benefits for more than one affected group. It also shows how a transition can lead into the effects, alternatives, or implementation of the proposed response. Preserve these analytical functions rather than reducing supporting sentences to a list of citations.

## 7. Make understanding the writer's responsibility — p. 9

Readers try to construct meaning by connecting ideas. When the text leaves a relationship unexplained, readers must pause and guess. They may supply a different interpretation, become confused, or stop reading. An implication that feels obvious to the writer is not necessarily evident to someone encountering it for the first time.

Treat English academic writing as reader-centred: make ideas clear, fluent, and easy to understand. The writer is responsible for providing the connections necessary for comprehension. The reader's speculative gap-filling example illustrates the risk; it is not a technique to imitate in an academic explanation.

Cohesion should connect ideas within and across sentences, paragraphs, and sections. It should clarify relationships, prevent disconnected statements, and sustain a logical progression. Apply the following four techniques, then check whether missing information requires a different repair.

## 8. Use linking words and transitions — p. 10

Signal the relationship between ideas explicitly. A link may express cause and effect, contrast, similarity, order, or addition. Choose a word or phrase that communicates the actual relationship, so that the reader does not have to infer why one statement follows another.

Original example:

- Disconnected: “The sample contains no weekend observations. The findings cannot describe weekend demand.”
- Connected: “The sample contains no weekend observations; therefore, the findings cannot describe weekend demand.”

The link makes an existing inference visible. It does not supply evidence for an inference that the material cannot support. Use a contrast marker for a contrast, an addition marker for another point, and a causal marker only when the causal or inferential relationship is established.

The reader recommends the Smart Words transition resource listed in section 18 for further relationship-specific connectors.

## 9. Present familiar information before new information — p. 10

Anchor new information in something the reader has already encountered. At sentence level, put familiar material near the start and the new material in the middle or at the end. Apply the same principle to how paragraphs and sections introduce new ideas.

Original example:

- Less connected: “The analysis identified three constraints. Staffing, storage, and delivery capacity were these constraints.”
- Better connected: “The analysis identified three constraints. These constraints were staffing, storage, and delivery capacity.”

The second version establishes the link immediately, then introduces the new details. The reader does not have to wait until the sentence ends to discover how those details relate to the previous statement.

## 10. Pair demonstratives with summary words — pp. 10–11

When using “this,” “these,” or “those” to refer to an earlier idea, pair the demonstrative with a meaningful summary word or phrase. Name the intended concept, event, finding, limitation, or action rather than leaving an unattended pronoun with several possible referents.

Original example:

- Ambiguous: “The team excluded responses from part-time students. This limits the conclusions.”
- Clearer: “The team excluded responses from part-time students. This exclusion limits the conclusions.”

Choose the summary noun according to the writer's actual intended referent. If that intention cannot be established, flag the ambiguity rather than guessing. Placing this demonstrative-plus-summary phrase at the start of the next sentence also creates a familiar-to-new progression.

## 11. Repeat key terms consistently — p. 11

Repeat words and phrases that identify the text's important concepts or themes. Such repetition gives the reader continuity and familiarity; it is not inherently dull. Synonyms can improve variety in other places, but unnecessary renaming of a central concept can imply that several different concepts are being discussed.

Original example: if “adaptive timeout” is a defined mechanism, do not alternate unpredictably among “waiting scheme,” “delay policy,” and “timing approach” as though they were interchangeable names. Keep “adaptive timeout” where conceptual identity matters. Vary wording only when the reference remains clear or a real distinction is intended.

## 12. Repair missing information, not just missing connectors — pp. 11–12, Table 1

The four cohesion techniques cannot repair every disconnection. Sometimes the reader lacks a fact, definition, intermediate idea, or relationship needed to understand the passage.

Locate the break, identify what information the reader needs, and insert that explanation when the supporting material establishes it. Adding a transition to an unexplained gap can leave the gap intact.

Two original examples preserve the contrast taught by Table 1:

- **Missing relationship signal:** “The device is suitable for indoor use. It has not been tested outdoors.” A contrast marker can clarify the connection without adding a new fact: “The device is suitable for indoor use. However, it has not been tested outdoors.”
- **Missing information:** “Users select inappropriate equipment. The indoor device should not be used outside.” The connection needs an established explanation that users are selecting this indoor device for outdoor tasks. If that fact is supplied, explain the selection, intended operating conditions, and resulting mismatch. If it is not supplied, identify the missing premise instead of inventing it.

Such gaps are common in drafts. Review from a first-time reader's perspective, and ask a peer to read the draft where possible. If the peer is confused or needs to ask a clarifying question, treat the location as a concrete revision signal.

## 13. Apply the reader's academic-style conventions — pp. 12–14

Academic readers assess writing partly by its observance of their field's conventions. Use the following recommendations as the reader's default style, while retaining the discipline-specific qualification in section 14. They are writing conventions, not claims that every alternative is grammatically incorrect.

### Avoid contractions

Write contracted negatives and similar abbreviated forms out in full in academic prose: for example, use “does not” instead of “doesn't.”

### Avoid unnecessary personal language

Replace personal-opinion framing and direct address with neutral statements appropriate to the evidence. Phrases that merely announce the writer's belief do not establish the proposition. Do not introduce an unsupported claim about what others believe while making the wording impersonal.

The reader makes an explicit exception: some disciplines accept “we” to describe what the researchers themselves did, found, or concluded. Do not use an undefined “we” to refer to the general public.

### Avoid informal sentence openings

The reader recommends avoiding sentence-initial coordinating conjunctions: **for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so**. Use an appropriate academic transition instead, or restructure the sentence to preserve the intended relationship. For example, an additive opening can use “In addition”; a contrast can use “However”; a supported consequence can use “Therefore.” A different assignment or publication convention may override this reader-specific preference; do not silently discard it by default.

### Prefer single-word verbs to informal multi-word verbs

Where the meaning is preserved, replace informal verb phrases with a precise single-word verb. For example, “carry out an analysis” can become “analyse,” and “look at the relationship” can become “examine the relationship.” The purpose is formal, precise expression, not arbitrary substitution with longer words.

### Prefer informative verbs to vague verbs

Replace vague uses of verbs such as “do,” “get,” and “make” with the action actually intended. Depending on the context, specify that researchers measured, obtained, constructed, calculated, or performed something. Preserve differences between actions rather than choosing a formal-sounding verb without checking its meaning.

### Recast direct questions as statements

The reader recommends statement forms instead of direct questions in academic prose. For example, “Can the intervention change participation?” can become “The question is whether the intervention changes participation.” Follow explicit assignment requirements when a research question must be presented as a question.

### Replace vague or unsupported evaluations with precise information

Identify what was measured and what the result was. Replace broad expressions such as a “large” number or a “good” outcome with concrete quantities or meaningful, supported criteria. If the evidence is absent, flag the missing information rather than manufacturing precision.

### Avoid “a lot” and “a lot of”

Use an appropriately formal quantity expression or, preferably when available, the actual number. “Many observations” is a register improvement; “48 observations” is more precise if 48 is established by the material. Do not invent a number to satisfy the style preference.

### Avoid exaggeration, overgeneralization, and unsupported certainty

Do not present a tendency as an absolute or guarantee success without supporting evidence. Use cautious language that accurately reflects the finding, such as a common tendency, a low likelihood, or a promising candidate. Qualify scope as well as certainty: specifying the tested conditions can be more informative than adding a vague hedge.

### Remove unnecessary wordiness

Prefer a short expression when it preserves the meaning of a longer phrase. For example, “in circumstances where” can become “when,” and “is able to” can become “can.” Inspect phrases expressing time, cause, conditions, capability, and purpose for avoidable padding. Concision must preserve the substantive point and its qualifications.

## 14. Learn the conventions of the discipline — p. 14

The reader's style list is not exhaustive, and conventions vary between fields. Be attentive to the actual discipline rather than assuming one list determines every acceptable choice.

Obtain and examine published articles in the relevant field. Compare their writing with the draft, identify the accepted style features, and determine which features should be adopted. Consult academic word lists to expand formal vocabulary, and academic phrase banks to learn conventional phrasing.

The reader distinguishes reusable, content-neutral academic phrases from an author's substantive ideas or distinctive expression. Conventional phrases can be adopted; the ideas and findings expressed through them still require appropriate attribution. A phrase bank does not provide evidence for an argument.

## 15. Attribute borrowed material — pp. 14–15

Acknowledge other authors' expert views, findings, and other material. Attribution applies to ideas as well as wording, and to illustrations, short sentences, small points, footnotes, appendices, and unpublished material. Small size or an obscure location does not make another person's contribution the writer's own.

The reader defines plagiarism as presenting another person's work or ideas as one's own. Its guidance covers quotation, paraphrase, summary, and a reference list or bibliography. Use the appropriate method of incorporating source material and provide its source; a bibliography does not remove the need to identify borrowed material in the text.

### Quotation

A quotation reproduces the source wording exactly. Mark copied wording clearly with quotation marks and attribution; do not pass altered wording off as a direct quote.

A short quotation can be integrated into a paragraph. A long quotation is normally set apart as a block with wider margins, following the required citation style's formatting rules. Use direct quotation sparingly in science and technology writing, including short quotations; the reader generally prefers paraphrase.

## 16. Paraphrase and summarize faithfully — pp. 15–16, Examples A–C

### Paraphrase

Restate the source using independently constructed vocabulary **and** sentence structure. Retain its main points and relevant details. A paraphrase may be similar in length to its source but is often shorter; aim for concision without losing the information needed for the task.

Copying a passage and changing a few words to synonyms is not adequate paraphrasing. The reader's Example A makes this distinction even for a short sentence. A citation does not by itself make close copying into independent paraphrase.

### Summary

Condense the source to its central points or main message. A summary is substantially shorter and omits detail that a paraphrase would retain. It still needs independent wording and sentence structure, and it still needs attribution.

### Make the boundaries of attribution clear

Ensure readers can identify where the borrowed account begins and ends. The reader's Example B illustrates this with attribution at the beginning and a citation at the end. Apply that clarity within the selected citation style rather than leaving the reader unsure which sentences come from the source and which contain the writer's analysis.

### Preserve message, tone, and emphasis

Compare the restatement with the source for more than word overlap. Retain the original meaning, main details appropriate to the operation, degree of certainty, tone, and emphasis. Independently phrased text can still misrepresent a source if it exaggerates or changes its position.

The reader's Example C teaches that a source raising a potential concern cannot faithfully be paraphrased as predicting an imminent crisis. A rewrite must pass both tests: independent expression and accurate representation.

### Take notes in a way that supports later paraphrasing

The source passage used in Example B also contains writing advice: excessive verbatim copying during note-taking can lead to excessive direct quotation in the finished paper. Limit exact transcription while taking notes, distinguish quotations from your own notes, and retain source information.

That illustrative passage mentions approximately 10% directly quoted material as a desirable ceiling in its own discussion. Preserve the underlying advice to limit quotation, but do not turn a percentage inside a paraphrasing example into a universal TU/e assessment rule. The reader's direct recommendation is to use quotation sparingly in science and technology and generally prefer paraphrasing.

### Original example: three different outcomes

Hypothetical source note: “During a four-week pilot, evening appointments were attended more frequently than morning appointments. The small sample prevents conclusions about annual demand.”

- **Paraphrase:** “The pilot's attendance records favoured evening over morning appointments, although its four-week duration and small sample leave yearly demand unresolved (pilot report).” The comparison and limitation remain, with a different sentence construction.
- **Summary:** “The pilot suggests stronger attendance in the evening, but cannot establish the year-round pattern (pilot report).” The main finding and qualification remain while detail is condensed.
- **Unfaithful restatement:** “Evening scheduling will increase attendance throughout the year.” This adds certainty and a time scope the source explicitly does not establish. Changing the wording does not repair that misrepresentation.

“Pilot report” here labels a hypothetical supplied source, not a real publication. In actual writing use the verified source's citation details; do not invent an author, year, or report.

## 17. Choose and consistently apply a referencing style — p. 16

Identify the style required by the assignment or commonly used in the discipline, and apply it consistently throughout the text and reference list. The reader identifies APA, MLA, IEEE, and Chicago as examples. It does not prescribe one universal style for all TU/e writing or teach all formatting rules for those systems.

Use the appropriate guide for details. When no requirement is given, preserve a clearly established style in the document; ask if choosing a style would materially change the requested output. Missing citation information should remain visibly unresolved until it can be established.
