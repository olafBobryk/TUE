# Academic writing review guidance

## Source and interpretation

Source: TU/e Language and Writing Center, *Academic Writing Reader*, 17-page edition supplied as university course material. Page numbers below refer to the reader's printed page numbers. No publication date is asserted because none was established from the supplied copy.

This guide is an independently worded adaptation for agent-assisted drafting and review. Examples below are original and hypothetical. The source PDF and its examples are not distributed here. This project is not affiliated with or endorsed by TU/e.

The reader describes useful conventions, including some prescriptive style preferences. Apply these in context: assignment requirements and disciplinary practice may justify a different choice. The agent-specific rules about preserving scope and handling unverified sources are implementation safeguards, not quotations or purported TU/e policy.

## Preparation and evaluation — pp. 3–4

Establish what the reader should understand or accept by the end of the text. Use that purpose to select material and organize the argument. Drafting may expose missing information, and evaluation may require returning to preparation.

Evaluate from the perspective of a reader encountering the material for the first time. Identify the exact point where the reader needs a definition, relationship, or explanation that the writer has assumed. Feedback is useful when it locates that gap and describes what is needed to close it.

## Argument and evidence — pp. 4–7

Check the connection between the overall message and each supporting argument. In argumentative passages, a claim should be contestable and specific enough to support. Descriptive or methodological passages need not manufacture a debate.

For each supporting argument, examine:

- **Claim:** what is being asserted, and under which conditions?
- **Evidence:** is the material relevant to this assertion, credible, and represented accurately?
- **Reasoning:** does the text explain the connection between the evidence and the claim, including limitations?

Evidence and reasoning can be interwoven after the claim. More citations do not compensate for missing reasoning. A source can be credible yet irrelevant to the particular assertion.

Original hypothetical example: a test reports lower median latency with caching. This may support a claim about typical response time under the tested workload. It does not establish higher reliability, reduced worst-case latency, or improvement under all workloads. Explain the supported connection and ask for the missing measurements if the broader claim matters.

## Paragraph structure — pp. 7–9

In a standard argumentative body paragraph, the opening topic sentence gives the claim, the supporting sentences supply evidence and reasoning, and the ending closes the point or prepares the next one. Usually one supporting argument is enough for a paragraph.

Check whether every sentence develops the paragraph's purpose. A paragraph containing two independent claims may need to be split. A closing sentence should do useful work, not mechanically repeat the opening. The reader explicitly notes that not every paragraph follows this standard model.

## Readability — pp. 9–12

Use four cohesion techniques where they improve comprehension:

- **Signal the actual relationship.** Choose transitions that match contrast, addition, sequence, or cause. “Therefore” must not create an unsupported inference.
- **Move from familiar to new information.** Begin from an established concept when introducing its consequence, property, or extension.
- **Clarify demonstratives.** Prefer a meaningful summary noun when “this,” “these,” or “those” has an ambiguous referent.
- **Repeat essential terms consistently.** Avoid decorative synonyms that suggest a different technical concept.

Original hypothetical example: “The client retries failed requests. This increases traffic.” If the intended referent is established, “These retries increase traffic” removes ambiguity. If it is unclear whether the writer means retries or failures, ask or flag the ambiguity rather than guessing.

When a reader cannot follow the connection because an intermediate fact or premise is absent, identify the missing information. Do not merely add a transition or invent the premise. Reviewers should describe the needed explanation; drafters and revisers can supply it only from established material.

## Academic style — pp. 12–14

Prefer concrete terms, informative verbs, and economical sentences. Replace vague evaluations with actual criteria or available measurements. Avoid unsupported absolute language; preserve the scope and uncertainty of the finding.

The reader generally recommends avoiding contractions, informal phrasing, unnecessary personal framing, direct questions, and certain sentence-opening conjunctions. Treat these as contextual style preferences rather than grammatical errors. Do not force awkward synonyms or passive constructions simply to appear formal.

The reader allows authorial “we” in disciplines where it describes what the researchers did or found. Distinguish this from an undefined collective “we” that claims to speak for everyone. Respect the assignment's or publication's convention.

Original hypothetical example: “The method always performs better” needs a supported comparison and scope. If the supplied results establish only a lower median error in three evaluated datasets, state that bounded finding without adding significance or generalizability claims.

## Attribution and source fidelity — pp. 14–16

Borrowed ideas, data, and illustrations need attribution, including when paraphrased or summarized. The reader distinguishes these operations:

- **Quotation:** reproduce wording accurately and mark it as a quotation, with a citation. The reader recommends sparing use in science and technology writing.
- **Paraphrase:** restate the relevant meaning and details using an independently constructed sentence structure and wording, with attribution.
- **Summary:** condense the main message, also with attribution.

Do not retain a source's sentence structure while swapping a few words and present that as an adequate paraphrase. Do not strengthen or weaken the source's position without justification. Keep the reader able to distinguish source findings from the writer's interpretation.

Follow the required citation style. If none is specified, use the document's established style when it is clear; ask when a style choice materially affects the requested output. Missing bibliographic details stay marked as missing rather than guessed. A plausible citation is not evidence of source verification.

## Further resources — p. 17

The reader points to university writing centers, academic vocabulary resources, and phrase banks. Those are optional learning aids rather than dependencies of this skill. Reusing a conventional academic phrase does not supply evidence or attribution for the substantive claim it introduces.
